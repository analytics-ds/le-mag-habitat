A water leak calls for a quick reaction to limit damage while waiting for a professional to intervene.

## In brief

1. Cut off the main water supply as soon as the leak is detected.
2. Cut the electricity if water is getting close to electrical installations.
3. Mop up and contain the water with buckets or towels.
4. Move moisture-sensitive items away from the affected area.
5. Identify the source of the leak when possible before the plumber arrives.
6. Contact a plumber quickly, even for a leak that seems minor.

## Cutting the water, the priority reflex

The very first action to take is cutting off the main water supply, usually accessible via a stopcock or directly at the meter. This simple gesture stops the leak from worsening and prevents more extensive water damage. It is worth locating this valve as soon as you move into a home, rather than searching for it in an emergency.

## How to find where a water leak is coming from

A visible leak (under a sink, around a seal, on an exposed pipe) is easy to spot. It is trickier for a hidden leak, for example in a buried pipe or one embedded in a wall. A localised damp patch on a wall or ceiling, a persistent musty smell, or an unusual rise in water consumption on the bill are telltale signs. To confirm a hidden leak, shut off every water point in the home and check the meter: if it keeps turning, a leak is active somewhere in the system.

## Securing the electricity if needed

If water is getting close to electrical outlets or plugged-in appliances, switching off the circuit breaker for the affected area becomes essential to avoid any electrical risk.

## Limiting damage while waiting for the intervention

| Action | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Mopping up water on the floor | Limit seepage into flooring |
| Placing buckets under the leak | Contain the continuous flow |
| Moving sensitive items | Protect furniture and electronics |

These simple steps, taken in the first few minutes, significantly reduce the extent of the material damage to be dealt with afterwards.

## Who pays for the repair, the landlord or the tenant

For a rental, how responsibility is split depends on the nature of the leak. Routine plumbing upkeep, such as replacing a seal or a hose, generally falls to the tenant as part of standard tenant repairs. Repairs linked to the installation's wear and tear or a structural fault (a failing embedded pipe, for example) fall to the landlord instead. In case of disagreement, the lease and the move-in inventory serve as reference to settle the matter.

## Water leaks and insurance, how it works

Water damage cover, included in most home insurance policies, allows for partial or full reimbursement of repairs and damage caused by a leak, depending on the policy's caps and deductibles. Reporting the claim to the insurer quickly, ideally with photos of the damage and the plumber's invoice, makes reimbursement easier. If the leak affects the common areas of a co-owned building, the building's own insurance may also be called on.

## Never underestimate a small leak

Even a discreet leak can seep into walls or under flooring with no immediate visible sign. This kind of damage can then encourage humidity, a problem to watch just as closely as [attic insulation](/en/blog/attic-insulation-where-to-start/) as part of a broader home maintenance approach.

## After the intervention, check the whole installation

Once the leak is repaired, it is worth checking the overall condition of the home's plumbing, especially if the installation is old. This vigilance is part of the broader approach of [organising everyday home life](/en/blog/organise-daily-home-life/).
